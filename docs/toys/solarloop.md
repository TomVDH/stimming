# Star catalog (`solarloop`)

Named Solar loop until 2026-11-24 (Tom renamed it); the folder and address stay `solarloop`.

Lab toy, idea 238. Bean `stimming-e35i`. Plan: `docs/plans/solarloop.md`.

A star's edge at a vast scale, graded like film. The look takes its cue from two kinds of film: the sun as a furnace, all gold and white light, and the slow, hazy, monumental frame of large-scale science fiction.
Seventeen stars (Tom, 2026-11-23): one for each class on the main sequence, O to M, two brown dwarfs, and the stages of a star's life, from a protostar to a pulsar. a ringed star (a model after J1407b's rings), Beta Lyrae, an interacting binary, S5-HVS1, a star thrown out of the galaxy, and a Wolf-Rayet star, WR 124, join the life stages, and after the pulsar a Thorne–Żytkow object, the 1977 prediction; two black holes and a quasar follow them: Cygnus X-1, Sagittarius A* and 3C 273 (Tom, 2026-11-24). Every star is seen from the side, north up, turning about its upright pole (Tom, 2026-11-24). The surface lives on its own: granules boil (each lives about 7 s; on the red supergiants, a handful of giant cells), swirls writhe over about a minute, the supergranule network creeps, dark filaments. Above the limb, loops of plasma arc on field lines and drain back. Three giant prominences stand over everything: a hedgerow, a forest of fine threads, a twisted arch, and a tall curtain. No other bodies, apart from Mercury in transit on our sun (Tom, 2026-11-23).
Three views, stops on one zoom (Tom, 2026-11-23): the wheel, a pinch or - and = glide through them (long lens, wide view, full star, then out), and L jumps in Tom's order: long lens, full star, wide view. Every visit opens far out, at x32, and glides in, slow and heavy, to the full star once the intro has gone; the card plays 2 s after the view rests (Tom, 2026-11-24). The long lens fills the frame with the star; the old quarter cut, mirrored onto the west side (Tom, 2026-11-24): its limb arcs from the foot's middle to the right side's middle, and the bottom right corner is open space; the camera holds still. The wide view stands back from the same limb, upright. The full view shows the whole star. The star is active all round its limb. A raymarched volume of haze and dust, lit by the star with Fire's smoke shading, wraps every star; Mercury crosses our sun's disk.
The film touch is light: fine grain, a soft colour split, a faint lens dirt, highlights that roll off to white.
Tap a region and it flares: two ribbons flash, the core burns white with an anamorphic streak and lens ghosts, a twisted rope lifts off as a bulb and leaves the frame, a bubble swells. A new arcade snaps in, cools and rains back. Tap a prominence and it erupts: it lifts and swells into space over about 15 s, then forms again.

- The first frame is a picture: every loop at its own stage of life.
- The view is orthographic. The wide view is 1040 star units across on a wide stage (760 at mid widths, 520 on a phone). It looks at the west limb from the side: the limb stands upright about a third of the way in from the left (Tom, 2026-11-24).
- The long lens is the default (Tom, 2026-11-23). It is 560 units across (420 at mid widths, 300 on a phone). The star fills the frame; its west limb arcs from the middle of the foot to the middle of the right side, and the bottom right corner is open space. The southern belt's regions and prominences stand on the arc's upper, equator end. Zooming from the long lens to the wide view stays on that limb. The camera never moves there. Loops and prominences draw brighter; the tone curve presses a little darker and harder; the vignette deepens.
- The full view fits the whole star with sky round it. The view pulls back to 3.2 radii tall. The corona is a raymarched 3D volume in the star's own frame, so it turns with the star: streamers gather on a warped sheet round the magnetic equator, narrow as they climb and bend in a slow spiral with height; polar plumes stand at the poles; fine rays run radially. It scatters the star's light with a Thomson lobe (brightest side on) and falls as the radius to the power 2.2 in density. The march reaches 3.4 radii. The exposure halves, so the whole disk shows its surface.
- Zooming out past the full star (x1 to x32), the star turns into one far light, with no seam (Tom, 2026-11-24): the corona, the haze, the loops and prominences, and everything round the disk fade out; the surface melts into an even glow; the star burns brighter; and the Buoy Business flare grows in round it: the disk shrinks to a hard white core in a round, globular glow (white at its heart, the star's colour farther out), a warm orb about twice the disk wide, a thin horizontal streak that tapers out before the frame's edge, and the ghosts (Tom, 2026-11-24). A sparse starfield in two layers comes through behind it, never over the disk; a drag slides it a little.
- Real light (Tom, 2026-11-24), after an HDR eye: up close the star is masked, so the corona, loops and surface detail show. From just past the wide view to about x16, the exposure rises up to 4.6 times, so the disk burns toward white and its fine detail washes out on its own; the light round the disk (the corona, loops, prominences, haze and the 3D corona) keeps only a quarter of its look against it; and the star's glare spreads past the limb and covers the corona. Past the full star that light fades on toward x32. The eye adapts late (Tom, 2026-11-24): every light value (the exposure, the glare, the far star's glow and flare, the off-disk weight) trails the zoom, about 2 s on the way in and 0.6 s on the way out. So a zoom in from far out overpowers for a moment: the glow, sized to the growing disk, and the flare balloon, then the exposure settles and the surface and corona show. The far streak reaches farther as the disk grows on screen, still ending inside the frame.

## The black holes (bean `stimming-mhpz`)

Event horizon's black hole, ported into Solar loop (Tom, 2026-11-24): Cygnus X-1, a stellar hole of 21 suns, and Sagittarius A*, the hole at the centre of our galaxy, 4.3 million suns.

- A hole has no surface: the edge of its shadow stands where a star's limb would. Its radius is R, so the hole's units follow from it: R is 2.6 Schwarzschild radii.
- The disk pass traces one light ray per pixel backward, as Event horizon does: RK4 on the bend of light near a Schwarzschild hole. The rays run parallel, so the hole is seen from far away. Past 40 Schwarzschild radii the rest of the bend is the weak-field term.
- The disc lies in the hole's equator, from the ISCO (3) to 14 Schwarzschild radii, tilted about 10 degrees toward the eye. Its far side arches over the shadow, a thinner arc runs under it, and each crossing adds light, so the photon ring forms. The gas turns at the Kepler rate in streaks that shear and reset unseen. The outer gas is thinner, so it never reads as dark blots.
- Gravity and Doppler shift the light (g to the fourth power). Hot, approaching gas takes the cool ramp: cream white on Sagittarius A*'s ember gold, ice blue on Cygnus X-1's lilac steel.
- Stars lie on a far plane behind the hole and are read along the bent ray, so they smear into arcs near the shadow. They come in two scales that cross-fade with the zoom.
- The long lens frames the shadow's west edge at the equator, where the disc crosses it; the wide view stands back from the same edge. The full view stands three times farther back than a star's, so the whole disc fits, and it keeps its full exposure. Far out the flare grows round the hole; its glow stays off the shadow until the shadow is a few pixels wide.
- No loops, prominences, haze, corona, heat shimmer or glare. A tap does nothing yet; dropping a star in comes next.

## The runaways (bean `stimming-q5aa`)

Two runaway stars (Tom, 2026-11-24: both).

- Zeta Ophiuchi, already on the main sequence row, is a runaway: as the view pulls back, its bow shock grows in ahead of it, a red arc of hydrogen and dust swept up by its wind, as Spitzer pictured it. The arc takes Wilkin's shape, r = R0 sqrt(3 (1 - t cot t)) / sin t, six radii out at its apex, brightest at the apex and rippled with filaments. It is not to scale: the true arc stands millions of radii out.
- S5-HVS1, a new object: an A star of about 2.35 suns that Sagittarius A* threw out of the galaxy's centre 4.8 million years ago; it moves at about 1,755 km/s and will not come back (Koposov et al. 2020). A long, faint, pale blue wake trails behind it, its knots streaming away; the wake is the toy's, not observed. Its surface takes Vega's look and ramps.
- The arc and the wake are drawn in the final pass in their own colours, after the grade, and never over the disk. Their heading hangs in space, so a drag turns them with the camera (Tom, 2026-11-24).

## The interacting binary (bean `stimming-t76h`)

Beta Lyrae (Sheliak), about 1,000 light years away (Tom, 2026-11-24): a blue-white giant, the donor, pours gas onto a heavier partner hidden in a thick disc. The pair eclipse each other every 12.94 days, and the period grows by 19 seconds a year as mass moves across.

- The donor is the toy's star. It turns once per orbit, so its partner holds still in the star's frame: one orbit takes about 112 s in the toy. The partner circles 5 donor radii out, wider than the true 3.9, so the band between them has room (Tom, 2026-11-24). The orbit tilts 24 degrees toward the eye, so the disc shows its face (Tom, 2026-11-24); the pair still graze each other at each conjunction, and whichever stands nearer covers the other.
- The gas is smoke, not a drawing (Tom, 2026-11-24): the disc and the stream are density in the raymarched volume, 48 steps, with Fire's K3 shading. The disc is about 1.8 donor radii wide and flat (Tom, 2026-11-24): thin inside, flaring a little toward a low raised rim where the stream piles in; its gas turns at the Kepler rate in streaks. Two tidal spiral arms, raised by the donor's pull, wind in toward the hidden star and hold still in the pair's frame. The inner disc burns hotter, brightest along the arms (Tom, 2026-11-24: so it reads as accretion).
- The stream follows gravity: a parcel of gas leaves L1, 1.77 donor radii out at this spacing, and falls through the pair's rotating frame (the restricted three-body problem, mass ratio 0.226, Coriolis and all) until it strikes the disc's rim. The toy traces it once with RK4 and draws a band along it (Tom, 2026-11-24: a connecting band): broad at the donor's neck, narrowing, then spreading where it meets the disc; it glows along its length, and clumps flow down it.
- Lit from within (Tom, 2026-11-24): the hidden star is a bright sphere at the disc's heart, and its light diffuses out through the gas, fading with the gas between, so the cloud glows warm round it and stays dark at its edges. The donor lights the gas only a little. The hot spot where the stream strikes glows on its own. The volume dims the sphere where gas lies in front.
- Eclipses come from the volume itself: gas in front dims the donor, and the march stops at the donor's surface, so the donor hides gas behind it. A faint jet with travelling knots rises from each face of the disc.
- The full view stands 3.1 times farther back, so the pair fits; the march reaches 7.6 radii. The donor's surface is a B giant's, blue white; the disc, the stream and the jets take the cool ramp, warm white. The donor is drawn round, not drawn out toward its partner as a Roche lobe would make it.

## The ringed star (bean `stimming-l4n7`)

A model (Tom, 2026-11-24: "ring is not around star", then rings round the star): a young K star with J1407b's giant rings set round it, Saturn-style. In nature the rings belong to J1407b, a companion that crossed the star V1400 Centauri for 56 days in 2007 and has not been seen since; the card says so. The tray calls it Ringed star.

- At least 37 rings with their gaps, the widest where a moon may sweep a lane, scaled to run from 1.5 to 6 star radii so the whole system frames; the bands keep their true proportions.
- The rings tilt about 17 degrees toward the eye, so they draw as an ellipse: the near half crosses the star's face and darkens it in bands, and the star hides the far half. Round the star they catch its light, brightest close in. They hang in space: the drag turns them with the camera, and the star's spin leaves them still.
- The full view stands 2.4 times farther back, so the whole system fits. The disk is an orange K star with spots; its loops and prominences are dim. The ring dust takes the cool ramp, amber tan.

## The Wolf-Rayet star (bean `stimming-gzrx`)

WR 124 in its nebula M1-67 (Tom, 2026-11-24): a hot, stripped star of 44,700 K, twelve suns wide, a runaway at about 200 km/s.

- Its own dense wind hides its surface: no granules, loops, prominences or spots. Clumps drift outward across the disk's face, and outside the limb the wind is a thick glow that falls as the radius to the power 2.5. Its clumps stream out as radial streaks, about 700 km/s.
- The nebula shows as the view pulls back: knots of shed gas in a shell about 9 radii out, crowded along one axis as in the JWST picture, glowing hydrogen pink. It is not to scale: the true nebula is about 45 million times the star's width. It hangs in space, so a drag turns it with the camera (Tom, 2026-11-24).
- The disk glows violet blue to white; the nebula takes the cool ramp, hydrogen pink.

## The Thorne–Żytkow object (bean `stimming-ye4c`)

A red supergiant with a neutron star sunk into its core, as Kip Thorne and Anna Żytkow predicted in 1977 (Tom, 2026-11-24). No object is confirmed: HV 2112 was recast as an ordinary giant in 2018, and HV 11417 may be a foreground star. So the tray holds the model itself, and the card says so: predicted in 1977, not yet found.

- Outside it is a red supergiant about 1,000 suns wide: a handful of giant cells, deep limb darkening, a dusty envelope, dim loops and prominences.
- A cutaway, always on (Tom, 2026-11-24): the envelope thins toward the middle of the disk, so the inside shows; the true envelope hides it completely. Within: a ragged burning shell about a third of the way in, brightest where its rim stands edge-on; a white-gold core zone; and the neutron star at the heart, a white-hot point with a halo, drawn far larger than its true 20 km. The core pulses slowly.
- The card rows status and core appear only on this object.

## The quasar (bean `stimming-vd4z`)

3C 273, the first quasar found (Tom, 2026-11-24): a hole of about 260 million suns, feeding hard. It is the black hole trace with a hotter, blue-white disc, and a jet.

- The jet leaves along the hole's spin axis a few radii out and opens about 5 degrees. Knots sit at even steps of log distance, so every zoom shows some, and they travel out. It fades slowly, so it reaches past any frame.
- Beaming: the jet toward the eye shines; the counter-jet behind the hole keeps 3 percent of its light. The jet's light is mostly the cool ramp, violet white.
- Far out it reads as its name says, quasi-stellar: a point of light with a jet, as the first plates showed it.
- Its horizon is about 1,100 suns wide, so it stands beside VY CMa in the tray.

## Eggs (bean `stimming-239r`)

A still press of 3 s on three stars plays a real event from that star (Tom, 2026-11-23). From 0.6 s the event builds; a release before 3 s runs it back out and does not flare. Each egg also plays on its own, about once per 25 minutes awake, but never under reduced motion. The code is in `eggs.js`.

|---|---|---|

Then two white-light kernels flash and part, as Carrington and Hodgson saw them by eye, and a large flare (1.6 times) rises from them. It has no sound of its own beyond the flare: the telegraph is gone (Tom, 2026-11-24) |
| Betelgeuse | The Great Dimming, 2019 to 2020 | A cool patch spreads over the southern half. Then a dust veil condenses over it and past the southern limb; the star falls to about 43 percent of its light, then clears by 36 s. The veil's noise sits in the star's frame. With sound on, a chord falls and muffles |
| Pulsar | Vela's true voice | The spin rises on a log scale from one turn in 1.6 s to Vela's true 11.19 turns a second; the beams and the field lines go with it. ## The model (lengths in star units; the star's radius is 696)

| Part | Model |
|---|---|
| Regions | Fourteen active regions, every one in a belt 5 to 35 degrees from the equator, as on the sun (Tom, 2026-11-24): five in the west limb's northern belt (the wide view), five in its southern belt (the long lens), four spread round the star; the star's turn carries them across the disk and round the limbs |

| Scale | Mercury is a grey shaded sphere lit from behind by the sun, with a thin sunlit rim. It crosses the disk level, east to west with the star's turn, a little south of the equator, on the long lens and the full view (Tom, 2026-11-24). No other star has a body beside it |

## Frame loop

- Lively while a flare, an eruption or an egg runs, a press is held, the zoom moves, a drag turns the star, a target's hover or press light eases or a star switch dips.
- Drifting at 30 fps: the loops' life cycle.
- Asleep 60 s after the last input: the drift eases to a stop over 2 s and the loop stops. A target turning under a still mouse is not input, so it never holds the toy awake.
- A star switch clears every flare and eruption at the dark point: the new star starts calm.
- Hidden tab: the loop and both clocks stop.
- Reduced motion: no drift; the loop sleeps at once. A tap still flares, softer (70 percent) and slower (80 percent); the shot noise holds still. The zoom and the star switch jump.
- Slow frames step down one rung, then two (`shared/detail.js`): a lower pixel ratio, then fewer bloom levels.

## Sound

Muted by default. Space makes no sound, so this is a sonification: each part has a visible cause and pans with its region.

- Awake, the star's voice in two layers (Tom, 2026-11-23). The star sets the key: the larger the star, the lower.
  - The space rumble: a constant, dense, deep surround drone, subtle (Tom, 2026-11-23). It renders once per star into a 24 s stereo loop whose last 3 s crossfade into its start, so it repeats with no seam: two independent deep noise beds, one per side, under twelve low sines round the root two octaves down, each placed across the stereo field. No swells: it holds steady.
  - The Sun's voice (Tom, 2026-11-24, set by ear in a tuning player), in A sharp: the Sun's key, so its brass blast and glow chord sit in A sharp too. A choir and a slowed choir, at half level each. The choir: one sawtooth wave per note, low passed at 3 kHz. The slowed choir: the same plus a wave an octave down, low passed at 1.8 kHz. Both pass four formant band passes (400, 750, 1150 and 2700 Hz). Three octave voices: the middle at 1, an octave down at 0.5, two octaves down at 0.6. Each wave sits at exact pitch, so nothing beats; one shared pitch drift of 3 cents at 0.2 Hz and a level swell of 20 percent at 0.12 Hz are the only movement. The chord holds the root, fifth, ninth and major third; every 10 to 12 s it crossfades over 4 s between Ionian (adds the major seventh) and major pentatonic (adds the sixth). Two top voices take turns: every 8 s the next note of the scale of the moment fades in over 2 s as the last fades out. A 7 s convolution reverb (wet 1.2, dry 0.5) replaces the echo hall. It fades in over 4 s and sits faint under the rumble.
  - Every other star (Tom, 2026-11-24): the same choir engine (`choir.js`), in the star's own key, shaped by its preset. A preset sets the source wave (saw, analog pad, organ, the old organ choir, reed, flute, pulse or FM), how much of the "ah" vowel shaping it keeps, the vowel, a ring-modulated metal copy, brightness, unison detune, synth resonance and filter sweep, the step and swell, distance, the echo (reverb, the hall or both), reverb length and mix, stereo width, the octave levels, the slowed choir and six sci-fi filters (machine comb, phaser sweep, screech, dark, transmission, deep echo). Each star's level has two parts (Tom, 2026-11-24): `NORM` in `choir.js` makes every star as loud as the Sun, measured offline, K-weighted as in ITU-R BS.1770, with the swell off and a fixed seed; `gain` is Tom's own level for the star on top, 0 dB by default. The lab measures one star again or every star, and writes the `NORM` table out.
  - Songs (Tom, 2026-11-24): each star has its own harmony (the Ionian and pentatonic blend, Ionian, pentatonic, harmonic minor or the harmonic series) and can have a progression: chords on scale degrees, each held for its length, round and round. Chords are triads, major, minor, sus 2, sus 4, add 9, 7th, power or cluster. A voicing spreads them (Sun, organ, open, close). Five note slots per octave take each chord; a note the next chord keeps holds still, the others dip and return or glide. The melody steps, glides or walks the chord as an arpeggio, on the chord's tones and scale steps between them. The library: Sun blend, Hold, Slow rise, Suspended drift, Lydian float, Ice, Ominous minor, Dark descent, Phrygian shadow, Overtones. The Sun keeps its blend, unchanged (a seeded render matches the old engine to 0.00000003). Starting songs: Lydian float or Suspended drift on the bright stars, Slow rise on Epsilon Eridani and Proxima, Ice on the brown dwarfs, Suspended drift on T Tauri, Ominous minor or Dark descent on Arcturus, Rigel and VY Canis Majoris, Overtones on the remnants; Betelgeuse holds the harmonic series.
  - The black holes and the quasar (2026-11-24) take the roots set in `sun.js`: Cygnus X-1 in G (98 Hz), glassy FM on the overtones; Sagittarius A* in E (82.41 Hz), the deepest, slowest analog pad in a Phrygian shadow; 3C 273 in D (73.42 Hz), a far, radio-loud pulse pad with a transmission filter. Gains match the Sun. A hole's full view stands 3 times farther back, so the zoom mix shifts its nearness by log 3 over log 16 / 1.4 to put that view on the full-star stop. Taps never fire on them.
  - WR 124, a Wolf-Rayet star (2026-11-24), in its `sun.js` root of 155.56 Hz: a bright, airy, very wide choir on the Lydian float, a slow phaser for its wind and long echoes for its shell of shed gas. Its gain matches the Sun.
  - The Thorne–Żytkow object (2026-11-24), in its `sun.js` root of 103.83 Hz: it starts from the quasar's sound, Tom's reference for strange bodies, made darker and heavier for the red supergiant, with a metal ring and a machine comb for the neutron star in its core and a slow swell for its faint pulse, on the Ominous minor progression. Its gain matches the Sun.
  - V1400 Centauri (2026-11-24), a young K star in eclipse behind J1407b's rings, in its `sun.js` root of 196 Hz: a warm choir, dimmed, on Slow rise; a phaser and a quicker swell for the ringlets that sweep past, a little icy metal and echoes. Its gain matches the Sun.
  - Beta Lyrae (2026-11-24), an interacting binary, in its `sun.js` root of 130.81 Hz: a bright choir on Lydian float, two chords that trade places for the pair; changed notes and the melody glide like the gas stream; the swell runs once per orbit, depth 0.35, so the choir dims with the eclipse: 112 s at the default x10k, and it keeps pace with the Turn speed (swTurn; main.js passes the eased rate to mixLayers); a little metal for the jets. Its gain matches the Sun.
  - S5-HVS1 (2026-11-24), the A star Sagittarius A* threw out of the galaxy, in its `sun.js` root of 311.13 Hz: a bright, glassy voice on Slow rise; changed notes glide, the melody is quick (5 s), a slow phaser for its speed and a long deep echo for its wake.
  - The Vela pulsar's choir sits nearer the quasar (Tom, 2026-11-24: less uncomfortable), as Tom set it in the lab: up 11 semitones to G♯5, the quasar's detuned pulse pad with filter swell 0.75, chorus and a loose 19-cent voice drift; a trace of metal and machine ring; no screech; more body in the middle and low singers; transmission, sweep and deep echo.
  - The pulsar (Tom, 2026-11-24: no ping, no notes, nothing that reads as a separate sound): each beam flash is a subdued highlight inside the neutron star's own choir. For about 0.3 s the choir opens (filter and vowel), bends 8 cents down and back, dips a little in level, and lifts its reverb into a short shimmer (wob, wobOpen, wobBend, wobTail).   - The zoom mix, one for all stars (`ZOOM` in `choir.js`): at each stop (far at 32 times, out at 4 times, the full star, the wide view, the long lens) a choir level, a rumble level and gains for the low, middle and high singers, the melody and the effects.
